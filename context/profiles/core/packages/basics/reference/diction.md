# Diction

Use precise technical terms when they do real work, and leave buzzwords unstacked. Prefer the plain word over the inflated one when both are exact. Write natural idiom with neither stiffness nor over-selling.

Buzzwords (avoid):

> I operationalize end-to-end ownership and deliver synergies across
> cross-functional teams.

Clear (better):

> I take responsibility for the whole chain, from design to operations, and
> get different teams pulling in the same direction.

Over-selling (avoid):

> A world-class, results-driven engineer passionate about cutting-edge
> innovation.

Natural (better):

> A developer who builds maintainable systems and ships them.

## Concrete over abstract when shortening

Shorten by choosing content away, never by replacing concrete words with formal abstractions. "The Slack flow" does not become "integrations" to save a line; when a simplification makes a sentence vaguer, cut the sentence instead. An empty intensifier gives way to the concrete noun ("backend services" where "solid backends" stood). See [claims_and_evidence.md](../../claims/reference/claims_and_evidence.md) § 7.

## Be as specific as the claim requires

A precision preference, with no word banned. When a text makes a claim about technology, name the system, layer, or practice the claim is actually about: a language model, a retrieval step, a recommendation system, speech recognition, an automated approval step, a trained classifier, a chatbot deployed by a named organization. The test is whether the claim could be checked. "AI decides" or "AI degrades" hides which system, which layer, and which people made the decision, so the claim cannot be checked; naming the layer makes it checkable.

The umbrella term is the right word when:

- the umbrella category really is the subject (a claim about the field, a policy that regulates the category, a public debate about it);
- the text discusses the term itself;
- it appears in a quotation or an official name (the EU AI Act, a job title, a programme name);
- professional copy uses it to introduce a familiar category, provided the text then says what the actual work is.

Vague (the claim is about a specific system but names none):

> AI is changing how institutions make decisions.

Specific enough for the claim:

> When a municipality lets a scoring model rank applications, the ranking
> decides who a caseworker reads first.

Category introduced, then made specific (professional copy):

> I build AI systems for document-heavy work: retrieval over contract
> archives, extraction with human review, and the evaluation that shows
> where the model fails.

The preference applies to all writing in every genre and language.
