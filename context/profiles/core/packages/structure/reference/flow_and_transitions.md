# Flow and Transitions

Flow is the property that the next sentence is where the reader
expected the document to go. It comes from ordering decisions made
before drafting — not from transition words sprinkled afterward.

## Order for the reader, not the chronology of writing

Sequence material by the reader's need: what they must know first to
make the rest load-bearing, then the load-bearing claims, then
qualifications. A working heuristic: draft the document as questions
a skeptical reader would ask in order, then answer them in that
order. Old-before-new is the local version of the same principle —
open each sentence with information the reader already holds and end
it with the new point, so the "known" half of one sentence hands the
"new" half of the next.

## Link sentences by echo

The cheapest transition is repetition with variation: end one
sentence on a concept and open the next by naming that concept.
"The merge failed because the branch was stale. The staleness came
from..." — the second sentence's subject answers the first sentence's
ending, and no connector is needed. This echo discipline scales up:
sections should open by connecting to where the previous section
landed.

## Use connectors precisely, not decoratively

"However", "therefore", "meanwhile" are logical operators — each
asserts a specific relation between the joined statements. "However"
claims contrast; if the two clauses do not actually contrast, the
connector is lying and a reader who trusts it misreads the argument.
"Therefore" claims consequence. "Additionally" adds weight without
logic. Choose the connector that names the real relation, or use the
echo technique and choose none.

## Signposting without formula

State the plan once where the reader needs it ("Three changes landed
this week; the schema change matters most"), then let structure do
the work. Formulaic scaffolding — a paragraph announcing what each
section will do, repeated in each section — is the written form of
clearing your throat. The strongest signpost is the first sentence of
each section carrying its own point; the second strongest is the
one-line roadmap placed exactly where the reader would otherwise
wonder what is coming.
