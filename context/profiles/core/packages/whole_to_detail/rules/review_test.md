# Review test

The whole-to-detail model ends in a practical check: five control questions that a reviewer asks of a draft, plus one symptom that most reliably exposes a fault in the order. Both come from §6 of the model in [whole_to_detail.md](../reference/whole_to_detail.md).

## The five control questions

1. Can the reader understand early what this part is meant to convey?
2. Does the reader have the concepts and prerequisites that what follows requires?
3. Is it clear why the details are there and how they fit together?
4. Is there too large a jump between the overall idea and the details?
5. Does the opening give a usable frame, or is it only a declaration of content?

## The late-relevance symptom

The most revealing sign is that the reader understands only much later why an earlier piece of information was relevant. The text may then need to move an explanation forward, make a relation explicit, or add a short orienting sentence — rather than add more background in general. This follows from the principle that the text must keep track, as it goes, of what the reader has been given the prerequisites to understand. [Heiser](https://gernot-heiser.org/style-guide.html)

## Applying the test

The questions apply at every level the model names: the whole document, each section, and each paragraph. Run them top-down. A document whose opening fails question 1 cannot be repaired paragraph by paragraph, because the paragraphs have nothing to attach to; fix the frame first, then descend.

At document level, the questions are asked of the summary or opening: does it state what the document is for and what it concludes, in terms the intended reader already holds? At section level they are asked of the section's first sentences and of the jump between those sentences and the section's details. At paragraph level they are asked of the core sentence: does it give a direction, so that what follows reads as refinement?

Question 5 is the one that catches content-declaration openings. An opening that only announces the topic gives the reader a label, and a label does no interpretive work; question 5 asks whether the opening gives a frame.

The late-relevance symptom is a reading test. Read the draft once, straight through, as the intended reader. Wherever you notice that an earlier fact only now makes sense, note the fact and the point where its relevance became clear. The repair is usually local: move the explanation forward, state the relation where the fact first appears, or add one orienting sentence. Adding general background is rarely the fix, and it often creates the repetition and detail problems listed in the anti-pattern catalogue.

A draft passes when every level answers all five questions and a straight read surfaces no late relevance.
