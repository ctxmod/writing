# Disclosing assistance

Whether to tell the reader that a text was drafted or edited with help, from a tool or a person, is decided in a fixed order. Binding policy comes first. Where nothing binds, the author weighs the reader's reasonable expectations against the evidence on how disclosure is received. Throughout, a statement about how a text was produced is never false. The ownership rules that hold whatever is disclosed are in [author_integrity.md](author_integrity.md) §6.

## 1. Binding policy first

A policy that governs the text applies whether or not the case seems to need it, as binding policy does throughout this module ([tests_not_bans.md](../../basics/reference/tests_not_bans.md)). The governing policy may be a publisher's, an institution's, an employer's, a platform's or the law's. The current ones differ in what they require, so the author reads the one that applies. Examples of the range, as of late 2026:

- In scholarly publishing, COPE and ICMJE require authors to disclose AI use and keep full responsibility for it. Springer Nature requires LLM use to be documented in the Methods section (or an equivalent part) and, on its main policy page, exempts AI-assisted copy editing for readability, spelling, grammar and tone from declaration; its separate guidance page and some journals ask for that declaration too, so the journal's own instructions decide.
- In news, the Associated Press's 2023 standards let staff experiment with generative AI but treat its output as unvetted source material and not as publishable content; later updates set disclosure standards where AI plays a material role in published content.
- In education, the University of Oxford's policy on AI in summative assessment (published July 2025, in force from the 2025/26 year) requires each assessment setter to state whether and how AI may be used, and requires students to acknowledge any use in the declaration format the setter prescribes. Other institutions differ, and course-level rules govern.
- In law, Article 50(4) of the EU AI Act, applicable from 2 August 2026, requires deployers to label AI-generated or manipulated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest. Text that has undergone human review or editorial control, with a person holding editorial responsibility for its publication, need not be labeled; superficial checks such as spell-checking do not count as review.

## 2. Where nothing binds

Where no policy requires a statement, the decision is the author's, and the evidence cuts both ways. In thirteen experiments with more than 5,000 participants, Schilke and Reimann (2025) found that disclosing AI use lowered trust in the discloser, through a loss of perceived legitimacy. Softer framings ("AI only proofread this") still lowered it, and trust fell further when a third party exposed the use instead. A review of 47 studies of news readers, summarized by Nielsen Norman Group in 2026, found no consistent reaction. Among the studies that isolated disclosure itself there was no consistent trust or credibility penalty. Other studies in the same summary found the penalty larger when AI generated the text than when it edited it, larger as the disclosed AI share grew, and largest for interpersonal writing. Nielsen Norman Group's PACED questions are a usable checklist for the decision. They ask about policy, audience, context (how much the text depends on sincerity, creativity or personal connection), the reader's expectations, and the degree of AI use.

Two findings settle the practical stance. A disclosure costs least when the text needs no excuse, because it meets every test in [author_integrity.md](author_integrity.md). And concealment that is later exposed costs most.

## 3. Silence is allowed, a false statement is not

Where nothing binds, a writer may keep public text silent about how it was produced. The `sales_outbound` profile records that a sender's policy of silence is compatible with assisted drafting (`outbound_principles` §7), and the general rule is the same. What is never allowed is a false or misleading statement about production. That includes claiming a text was written personally, by hand or from scratch when it was not, describing heavy generation as "light editing", and denying assistance when asked directly. A false statement about how a text was made is a false claim, and false claims have no exception in this module.

## 4. Genres where the reader's expectation decides

In some genres the reader takes the author's own effort, attention or feeling to be the point of the text. Condolences, personal thanks, apologies, references written for a named person and work submitted for assessment are the clear cases. There, [author_integrity.md](author_integrity.md) §6 keeps assistance at the level of editing, so no disclosure question arises. When more help than that was used in such a genre, the reader's reasonable expectation is that they will be told, and a binding assessment policy usually says so explicitly.

## How to apply

Before drafting, find any policy that governs the text and follow it (§1). Where none does, decide with the PACED questions and record the decision where the consuming repository records such things (§2). Before sending, check that nothing in the text, its signature or its metadata misstates how it was produced (§3), and that a personal genre was written by its author (§4).
