# Author integrity

A reader gives a text the trust they give the person behind it. They assume the author means what it says, knows what it claims, and would answer for it if asked. Integrity is the author's side of that bargain. Every sentence belongs to the author, in substance and in wording, whoever helped produce it. The reader-side discipline is in [reader_empathy.md](../../reader/reference/reader_empathy.md); this file holds the author side, and [disclosing_assistance.md](disclosing_assistance.md) holds the question of when to say that help was used.

## Why the stand_behind_every_sentence rule holds

Readers trust a text as far as they trust the person they believe stands behind it, and the first follow-up question exposes a sentence nobody owns. Borrowed experience and performed feeling fail at once when noticed, and a reader who suspects that a text was generated on the sender's behalf discounts both text and sender.

## 1. Stand behind every sentence

The test for each sentence: the author would say it, in these words, to this reader in person, and could answer a follow-up question about it. A sentence that passes is the author's even when someone else drafted it. One that fails is a liability even when it is true, because the reader will hold the author to it, and the first follow-up question will show that nobody stands behind it.

The test generalizes two rulings this module already holds. AI output is an editor's input, and the final text keeps only wording the author would say aloud ([tone_and_register.md](../../basics/reference/tone_and_register.md), "Against the template voice"). And a list entry is a promise to answer three follow-up questions about it ([show_qualities.md](../../claims/rules/show_qualities.md)). Both extend to every sentence of an outward text.

## 2. Write from what you know

An outward text says what the author observed, did, decided or believes, and keeps those apart (statement types in [claims_and_evidence.md](../../claims/reference/claims_and_evidence.md) §2). Experience in the text is the author's own. Borrowed experience fails without exception, because its whole effect depends on the reader believing it happened. That covers an invented anecdote, a composite story told as one event, "a client once told me" with no client behind it, a testimonial written for someone else to sign, and an opinion adopted because it sounds informed.

In some settings the rule is also law. The US Federal Trade Commission's rule on consumer reviews and testimonials (16 CFR Part 465, in force since 21 October 2024) prohibits reviews and testimonials that misrepresent the reviewer's experience, explicitly including AI-generated ones and those from people with no actual experience of the product. The writing rule is wider than any such law and holds in every genre.

Someone drafting on the author's behalf (an assistant, an agent, a colleague) cannot know the author's experience. They ask, and where no answer comes, they leave the gap visible and flag it, as the personal-contacts rule of the `correspondence` profile already requires for shared history. A plausible detail supplied to fill the gap is an invented one.

## 3. Honest uncertainty

Uncertainty is stated where it is, in the sentence it concerns, and nowhere else ([claims_and_evidence.md](../../claims/reference/claims_and_evidence.md) §3; for messages, `message_doctrine` §6 in the `correspondence` profile). There are two opposite failures. Hedging everything protects the author at the reader's expense, because the reader can no longer tell which claim is solid. Fluent confidence beyond the evidence borrows authority the author does not have, and it fails when the reader checks. "I do not know", "I have not tested this" and "this is my reading, not a finding" are sentences an honest text can contain.

## 4. A voice the author owns

The voice of a text is what it notices, what it chooses to say and how it says it ([tone_and_register.md](../../basics/reference/tone_and_register.md), "Self-labels"). A voice is the author's when the author could sustain it in conversation with the same reader. A letter in a register its author cannot speak sets up a disappointment at the first call or meeting, and a text whose voice changes from one paragraph to the next tells the reader that more than one hand wrote it without one hand owning the result. The voice stays consistent across the channels the reader may compare, as the message doctrine's credibility rule requires (`message_doctrine` §8 in the `correspondence` profile). A personal or house style layer may fix how the voice builds flow; this module asks only that it be one voice, and the author's.

## 5. Warmth without fake intimacy

Warmth comes from a real relationship and from specifics. It shows in the shared history that actually exists, the detail that shows attention, and a tone the author would use in person (the inner-circle register in `messages` of the `correspondence` profile is the worked form). Fake intimacy imitates the signals without the relationship. Typical forms are first-name chumminess with a stranger, values claimed in common with no ground, feelings reported at a strength the author does not feel ("I was so moved by your post"), and enthusiasm turned up to stand in for a reason.

The test: is the feeling true, and is it the size the author would show face to face? When it is, state it once and plainly, and let the specific detail carry it. When it is not, cut it. A calm, specific text reads as warmer than one that announces its warmth.

## 6. Assistance and ownership

Tools, editors and colleagues may help with research, structure, critique and wording. Ownership does not move with the help. The author owns the facts, the interpretations, the choice of reader, the wording and the decision to send or publish. The `sales_outbound` profile states this for sales messages (`outbound_principles` §7), and the rule holds for every outward text.

Publishing ethics reaches the same position from the other direction. The Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE, 2023), the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) and Springer Nature hold that an AI tool cannot be an author, because authorship carries accountability for the work and a tool cannot take it. The human authors answer for every part of the work, including the parts a tool produced, and are expected to check that output for errors, omissions and bias.

Ghostwriting shows where assistance stays honest. A speechwriter, an editor or a communications team drafting for a named author is a long-standing practice. It stays honest on two conditions. The named author reviews and adopts every word and answers for it. And the genre's readers know that such help is normal, as with a head of state's speech, a company's statement or an edited book. It stops being honest where the reader values the text for the author's own effort, attention or feeling. That covers a condolence, a personal thank-you, an apology, a reference written for a named person, and work submitted for assessment. In those genres the words are the author's own, and assistance stays at the level of editing (spelling, a word, a sentence untangled). The evidence that readers discount such texts when they suspect a machine wrote them is in [why_machine_text_fails.md](../../machine_tells/reference/why_machine_text_fails.md).

## How to apply

Run §1 on every sentence of an outward draft before it is reported done, and §2 on every anecdote, example, testimonial and first-person claim. Run §3 wherever a hedge or an unqualified claim appears, §4 on a straight read for voice breaks, and §5 on every sentence that reports a feeling. Settle §6 before drafting, when the author decides what help to use; whether to say so is decided under [disclosing_assistance.md](disclosing_assistance.md).
