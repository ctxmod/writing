# Substance — shown qualities and defensible claims

Applies to any claim an author makes about themselves, their work, or the world.

## Showing a quality

Show a quality through context, action, or result, and let the reader infer it. A quality adjective ("driven", "structured", "rigorous") is allowed only when the evidence for it is given right next to it, in the same sentence or the next one; an adjective standing on its own is a claim without support.

Weak:

> Structured and thorough.

Stronger:

> Introduced a shared code-review routine the team now follows.

Also fine (the adjective sits on its evidence):

> I introduced a shared code-review routine, a structured habit the team
> still follows.

Interest and appreciation are shown the same way: a concrete detail of the thing carries them, and generic enthusiasm gives way to it.

## Defensible claims

- Every claim rests on context, action, or result; without evidence, cut or soften it.
- Honest direction — an ambition, where one is heading — is allowed when it is framed as direction, never as something achieved.
- Never inflate and never invent.

## Only list what you can defend

A list entry is a promise to discuss the thing. The test is "could I answer three follow-up questions about this?", with the counter-question "do I know something about it" carrying no weight. One thin entry spreads doubt across every other entry, so cut rather than soften: a hedged list entry still reads as a claim. Awareness-level interest belongs in prose as direction, or in private development notes.

## Quantify when honest

Use a number whenever a real, defensible one exists; when none does, describe scope and outcome in words rather than padding with a vague figure.

## Applies beyond the self

For essays and analysis the same discipline governs claims about the world: a claim is supported, delimited, or marked as the author's interpretation. See [claims_and_evidence.md](../reference/claims_and_evidence.md).
