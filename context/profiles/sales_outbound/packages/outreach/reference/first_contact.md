# First contact

A first sales contact reaches a recipient who did not ask for it. It applies correspondence §2 and §4 ([message_doctrine.md](../../../../correspondence/packages/message_doctrine/reference/message_doctrine.md)) to a cold opening: the message opens with why the topic concerns the recipient, and everything after earns the next sentence. This file holds the consolidated cold-email craft and the first-contact rules of a sales team's communication principles. A first contact made through a referral also follows [introductions_and_referrals.md](introductions_and_referrals.md).

## The opening carries information for the recipient

The first sentence carries the observation, the answer or the decision most relevant to the recipient. A sender-centric opening fails: one that leads with what the sender now does, offers or is, through which company, when no shared context makes that relevant. The repair changes the information the opening carries; a more elegant synonym for the same information repairs nothing, and the pronoun "I" decides nothing on its own (correspondence §2).

Opening with the sender's activity (avoid, illustrative):

> I now take on delimited development assignments through my company.

The repair carries the recipient's situation instead. What fills it depends on the groundwork, so the sketch shows only the shape:

> [A verified observation about the recipient's situation], [and the part of it that concerns what the sender can do].

Exception: a former colleague or contact who has asked what the sender does next has asked for exactly that information, and then it opens the reply; a problem-based opening is not forced on a recipient who never asked for one. The exception is a case, never a default.

A short sender introduction may follow the opening when it helps the recipient judge the message. It never leads. The company need not be named in the body; the signature carries name, company and contact details, plainly, without slogans, title piles or competing links. The commercial purpose stays unmistakable (principle 6 of [outbound_principles.md](outbound_principles.md)).

## The subject line classifies the content

The general subject-line rule in correspondence `messages.md` applies: the subject line states the message's intent and never only a territory. A sales first contact specialises it, because its intent is to raise a concrete subject matter with the recipient: the subject line names that subject matter at a technical depth that fits the recipient. Plain, un-clever subjects of the "Regarding your AI initiative" class pass; wordplay fails. A false "Re:", invented urgency, a vague promise of large savings, or a subject that looks like it comes from an existing customer fails, because an opening built on a misunderstanding is not a successful first step.

## The four-part shape

A first cold email has one person, one observation, one offer and one easy out, in four or five sentences:

1. The hook, one sentence: a true, specific observation about the recipient, drawn from the research. It shows the message was written for them and earns the next sentence.
2. The bridge, one or two sentences: the hard part of their situation, named as a hypothesis (principle 5 of [outbound_principles.md](outbound_principles.md)), and in a clause the specific competence that bears on it. Credibility lands here, concretely, as a thing the sender has done or knows, never as adjectives.
3. The offer, one sentence: something useful, low-commitment and optional, framed as help — a short call, a specific thought, a look at their setup, a relevant work sample.
4. The out, one short close that makes ignoring the message easy. It is confidence, and it doubles as the opt-out the consuming repository's compliance controls require.

Then the plain signature. A first touch carries no pitch paragraph, no feature list and no attachments.

The shape is a default for the cold email with a real hook, and the tests behind it govern where it does not fit. When the groundwork is weaker, the section below applies. When the recipient's situation makes a direct offer relevant, the offer may come earlier; a technical observation is not a mandatory entry ticket.

## Personalization is the product

A template sent as it stands is spam; the same structure filled with one recipient's real specifics is a personal letter, and the difference lies entirely in the research. Three tests apply:

- The hook is verifiably true and specific to the recipient. "I saw you're hiring an engineer to take your support assistant to production" is a hook; "I love what you're building" is filler. Every claim the message rests on traces to something real, and a fabricated or careless observation is worse than no contact.
- The bridge shows domain understanding of what actually goes wrong in situations like theirs, and a generic "this is hard" fails it.
- The message names the recipient's stack, sector or constraint, and the sender's competence appears only as the reason the sender can help with it.

The swap control decides whether the personalization does work: if the observation were replaced by another company's, would the rest of the message need to change? When it would not, the personalization is decorative. The detail used is the one that actually changes the proposal or the question; a message that shows the sender did research without that research changing anything fails. When the research yields no real, specific hook, the recipient is not ready to be contacted, and the draft goes back to research; a weak email is never padded.

## Weaker groundwork

When no observation carries a full hook, a first contact may start from a concrete verified fact and a question that genuinely decides whether a relevant problem exists. The message says why the sender asks, and claims no solution it has not found. The sender keeps a reasonable bound on unpaid preparatory work, per the early-economics rule in [credibility_and_offers.md](credibility_and_offers.md).

## The hard lines

Each of these is a test the draft passes or is rewritten for:

- Lead with the point: no warm-up ("I hope this finds you well", "I'm reaching out because") and no restatement of what the recipient's company does.
- Their pitch is not echoed back: they know what they build, so it is acknowledged in a clause at most and the message moves to substance (correspondence `messages.md`).
- Every adjective the sender would apply to themselves is replaced by a concrete thing built or a result, per [credibility_and_offers.md](credibility_and_offers.md).
- The offer is named plainly and the message stops: no urgency, no artificial scarcity, no asking twice.
- An artifact that carries the proof (a work sample, a demo, a page) is linked once and the note stays light; the sender's fit is not recited.
- One clear next step, and a step that is the recipient's to take is left with them.
- No flattery, no hype and no exclamation marks: calm and specific reads as senior, enthusiastic and broad reads as junior or as selling. The warm register of a referred first contact is the one exception: its greeting may carry an exclamation mark, per correspondence `messages.md` and [introductions_and_referrals.md](introductions_and_referrals.md).

## Length and form

A first email runs to about 60–110 words; a longer one is doing too much. A composed short draft of a different artifact class, such as an email fallback written alongside a call script, may carry a tighter bound set by the consuming repository; the 60–110 band governs a first email drafted from research or from a scaffold. The message is plain text, not HTML, and looks like a person typed it. It is written in Swedish to Swedish recipients and in English otherwise, unless the consuming repository's language policy says otherwise; Swedish drafts follow `talspraksnara_utskick` (module `writing_sv`, profile `korrespondens`, package `saljutskick`). An English draft takes the same plain spoken register: sentences the sender could say aloud with the same words, one ask, no marketing words and no constructions built for rhythm. The clause-built register of the English style preference governs the owner's other English text and not a written outbound draft. It is written in the first person and is calm, and it is personal correspondence, never marketing copy.

## Worked contrast

The contrast is anonymized and carries no real sender's facts; names, the company and the domain are illustrative.

Selling (avoid, illustrative):

> Hi! I'm an experienced consultant passionate about helping companies unlock the power of data. I'd love to hop on a quick call to explore how I can add value to your team!

Professional (the target, illustrative):

> Hi Maria, I saw that Northwind is hiring an engineer to bring its invoice-matching pilot into production. In projects like that, the part that tends to bite is the long tail of supplier formats once the pilot data is replaced by the real inflow — that is the kind of work I do, and I have built matching pipelines for messy supplier data before. If it would help, I'm happy to spend 20 minutes on what usually goes wrong at that step and how to catch it early. If the timing is off, just say so.
>
> Anna

The failing version opens with the sender's identity and adjective credibility and asks for a meeting. The working version has the same four parts: it opens with a verified observation about the recipient, names the hard part as what tends to happen in such projects, grounds the competence in a thing done, offers something useful and optional, and leaves an easy out. The credibility clause states work the sender has done; a sender with verifiable career history may carry it there under the rule in [credibility_and_offers.md](credibility_and_offers.md).
