# Prose Shapes

Three skeletons cover most essays and articles: thesis-led,
narrative-led, and problem/solution. The shape is chosen for the
material and the reader, before drafting — a piece that changes
shape mid-write reads like two pieces stapled together.

## Thesis-led

The workhorse of argument. The claim comes early (after the
concrete opening), the body marshals the support in order of
strength or of the reader's likely objections, and the close lands
the implication.

1. Concrete opening that plants the question.
2. The thesis, stated in one sentence a stranger could repeat.
3. Support, strongest first or objection-ordered: each unit a
   claim, its evidence, its tie back to the thesis.
4. The honest counter-argument and the answer to it — where the
   reader's skepticism actually lives, not the straw position.
5. Close: the implication of the thesis, not a recap.

Fits: opinion and analysis pieces, technical arguments, reviews.
Risks: the middle section listing evidence without arguing
(annotated bibliography syndrome).

## Narrative-led

The material is a story, and the argument arrives through it. The
opening scene carries the stakes; the middle follows the chronology
or the investigation with tension held by what the reader does not
yet know; the turn delivers what the events meant; the close
returns to the opening scene changed.

1. Opening scene, concrete, in motion.
2. Rising complication: what happened, with the uncertainty visible.
3. The turn: the discovery, decision, or reversal.
4. The meaning — stated, not implied to death.
5. Close on the opening's material, transformed.

Fits: reported pieces, case studies, origin stories, post-mortems
with a human axis. Risks: plot without point — events narrated with
the meaning held for a final paragraph that never comes.

## Problem/solution

The reader lives with the problem, so the problem is rendered first
— specific, costly, familiar — and the solution is presented as the
reader's own next move, with its limits owned.

1. The problem, made concrete and expensive (what it costs, who
   pays).
2. Why the obvious fixes fail — one paragraph, so the reader trusts
   the solution was not chosen in ignorance.
3. The solution, in the reader's terms: what to do, what it
   requires, what it does not fix.
4. The first step, small and today-shaped.
5. Close on the problem solved or the cost of waiting.

Fits: advocacy, product and policy writing, how-think pieces.
Risks: the problem inflated past the solution's size, which reads
as salesmanship.

## Choosing

The material usually announces the shape: if the piece's power is in
what happened, narrative-led; if in what is true, thesis-led; if in
what should change, problem/solution. Mixed shapes exist for long
forms — a narrative-led piece whose final third argues the thesis —
but a mixed shape is a designed structure, and the seam must be
deliberate: the story's turn is where the thesis statement goes, so
the piece changes gear exactly once, at a joint the reader feels as
purpose.
