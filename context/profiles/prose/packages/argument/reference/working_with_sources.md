# Sources — tracing, weighing, and summarizing evidence

Applies whenever a text rests a factual claim on sources: essays, analysis, criticism, proposals, reports. [claims_and_evidence.md](../../../../core/packages/claims/reference/claims_and_evidence.md) governs how a claim is scoped and calibrated; this file governs what the sources behind it actually establish. How a contested claim is argued is in [argumentation.md](argumentation.md).

## 1. Count origins

Test: for a claim supported by several accounts, trace each back to where it comes from. Accounts that depend on the same recollection, press release, or earlier article are one piece of evidence, however many times it was published. The useful next check is the origin (correspondence, an acquisition record, a documented interview, scholarship that cites such material); another article repeating the claim adds nothing.

> Illustrative: four separately published accounts of a 1970 refusal may depend on the
> same family recollection, and a later review may report it without identifying a
> contemporary document.

The origin need not be the involved party's own account: a properly documented scholarly account suffices. Independent confirmation is the goal; institutional confirmation is neither required nor sufficient.

## 2. Name who supplies the evidence

Test: the attribution lets the reader see who supplies the claim and on what basis. "According to several sources" obscures; "according to the family's later accounts" or "according to X's interview with Y" shows. An explicitly attributed report can stay in the text when its weight is visible.

## 3. Absence in what you consulted is a limit of your search

Test: check whether the sentence reports what you did not find, or implies that someone else withheld something. "As far as is known, the museum has not itself accounted for the matter" risks suggesting institutional concealment when what was established is a limit in the material consulted. State the limit as yours, or leave it out of the main text. And the absence of an account does not show that the event did not happen.

## 4. A source supports what it says, no more

Test: read the cited passage against the exact sentence it is attached to, element by element: date, endpoint, count, agent. A note that says performances continued after a death does not support "until around 1800".

The same check applies before narrating a transition: look for the primary record that could contradict the story. Moderna Museet's own chronology shows an af Klint exhibition in 1989–1990, so "finally showed her in 2013" would be wrong, and the correction improves the argument: recognition became a changing institutional judgment instead of one dramatic turn.

## 5. Resolve a conflict on the evidence

Test: when two sources disagree, ask whether you would have chosen the same one had the other suited the argument better. Where you would not, the choice is convenience. Document the disputed point independently, prefer the route that settles it (a critical edition that prints both versions over two secondary summaries side by side), or remove the unsupported detail and say only what both support.

## 6. Remove the unsupported claim along with its detail

Test: after a detail is dropped for lack of support, check whether the sentence still asserts the same unsupported event. Dropping the year from a refusal leaves the refusal. Verify the episode or omit it; consider first whether the argument needs it at all.

## 7. Summaries keep the source's degrees of confidence

Test: compare the summary with the source's own gradations (firm, tentative, disputed; all, many, some). A summary may shorten; it may not collapse those grades into one, in either direction.

> Illustrative: a catalogue lists more than 73 works and firmly attributes about 45;
> other entries are tentative or concern works still to be located. Prose that says
> "attributed more than seventy", and a verdict of "twice as many", collapse the grades.
>

Quantifiers follow the evidence the same way: "most" is a claim about a majority and needs evidence of one; "many" claims less and is the right word when that is all the text knows. The grade belongs in the prose as well as in any note: a reader who never opens the note should not be misled. Where the gradation is the point of a table or data exhibit, it is shown there instead.

## 8. Prefer the narrower causal claim

Test: for every "without X, Y would not have …", ask what evidence shows that no other route existed. Usually there is evidence for what did happen ("this performance depended on these decisions") and none for the counterfactual ("without these people the work would not have reached us"). Write the narrower claim unless the stronger one is documented.

> The chain that produced the 1829 performance is not automatically the
> chain on which the work's survival depended: Mendelssohn's score was a
> copy of one in Zelter's collection. That documents a route to
> performance, and no archival single point of failure.

The same applies to rescue narratives: check whether the work was already known, performed, or read before the advocate's intervention.

## How to apply

Run 1–3 on every claim resting on reports rather than documents; 4 on every footnote, at the level of each element of the sentence it supports; 5 whenever sources conflict; 6 after every cut made for lack of support; 7 on every number, quantifier, and summary of someone else's findings; 8 on every counterfactual and every rescue or turning point.
