# Argumentation — contesting, testing, and concluding

Applies to any text that argues against a claim, tests a thesis against cases, or evaluates a work: essays, analysis, criticism, proposals, and any review of another author's argument. [claims_and_evidence.md](../../../../core/packages/claims/reference/claims_and_evidence.md) governs the author's own claims; this file governs claims made about and against someone else's, and the shape of the conclusion. How evidence from sources is traced and summarized is in [working_with_sources.md](working_with_sources.md).

## 1. Contest the claim the author actually makes

Test: point to the passage where the author says what you dispute, and check that your paraphrase says no more than that passage. Where the paraphrase is stronger, the author can concede your whole argument and keep theirs.

> Illustrative: a critique of Paul Graham's essay on taste states his position as "time itself sorts".
> His passage concerns the likely durability of _appeal_, and makes no
> guarantee that worthy works will be preserved, correctly attributed, or
> found.

Engaging a stronger version is legitimate when it is marked as an extension and left unattributed: "He could also reply with something stronger: …" passes; a stronger version stated as what he said fails. Recognizing that you may be reading a theory into practical advice does not license the stronger reading afterwards.

## 2. Put the disagreement where it decides something

Test: ask whether the opponent could accept everything your evidence shows and keep the thesis. Where that is possible, the disagreement sits in the wrong place. Find the distinction on which the two positions actually part, and keep what you grant visibly separate from what you contest.

> A critique that sets time against people misses: Graham can accept that people carry
> works without giving anything up. The deciding distinction is between
> longevity as _evidence_ and longevity as a _verdict_.

A generous reading helps here rather than softening the critique: naming what the opposing text achieves ("a practical essay about making things") and what it does not demonstrate ("that aesthetic judgments converge on timeless answers") narrows the disagreement to the claim that matters.

Over-correction is the same failure in reverse: the critique must stop where its evidence stops ("historical reception tells us nothing" concludes more than such a critique can carry).

## 3. Underargued is not contradictory

Test: before calling passages incompatible, ask whether one person could hold them all at once without inconsistency. Where they could, the fault is a missing argument: name the premise the author needs and has not supplied, and work there.

> Good work arises in particular communities; contemporaries can be wrong;
> appeal across very different periods can be evidence of merit. These are
> compatible. The missing argument is why historical transmission should
> make those judgments independent or reliable enough.

Where there is a real contradiction — the text asserts and denies the same thing — say so plainly. Words like "chafes" or "tension" are fine when the sentence then says what is missing.

## 4. A test must be able to fail, and fail fairly

A criterion proposed against a view has three requirements.

1. It can come out against you. "That people carry works is obvious, and it disproves nothing" is the right instinct: an observation the opposing view already accepts cannot test it.
2. Its conditions are ones the opponent would accept as relevant. Do not build it out of necessary conditions whose failure you then count as disproof. Ask of each condition: would a careful defender of the view accept it as necessary? And ask of each finding: does the opposing view itself predict it? A finding the view predicts cannot refute it.
3. It asks about reliability, in degrees. Phrase criteria as questions (how dependent, how vulnerable, how open to revision), never as pass/fail gates.

> Illustrative criterion: an independent verdict requires that survival did not hang on a few
> people's choices, and that the carriers' mistakes did not shape what
> survived. Neither follows: one person can preserve a work for excellent
> reasons, and a large network can perpetuate one unexamined judgment. And
> later correction of mistakes is what a defender of cumulative judgment
> expects.

A test counts only for what actually faced it. Positive evidence can be real without its absence counting as negative evidence: "A work nobody encountered did not fail a test of taste."

## 5. Keep the case that resists your thesis

Test: before dropping or replacing a case, ask whether you would still do it if the replacement gave a less favorable result. Where the only gain is a cleaner victory, keep the original. A test that retains the case most favorable to the opposing argument is more credible.

Replacing is right when the case is factually unusable or does not test the thesis. Adding a case is a separate decision, and usually the wrong one when the argument is being criticized: it needs clearer limits more than more examples. A candidate case brings its own discipline: it must not become another tidy rescue story either (Schubert's C-major symphony, often told as rescued from oblivion, had a rehearsal reading in his lifetime).

## 6. Keep the kinds of judgment apart

Three kinds of judgment are shown wrong in different ways, and they are not interchangeable examples of "correction":

| Kind                      | What would show it wrong                                                                               | Example                      |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------- |
| Factual                   | Evidence: a document, a record                                                                         | Thoré's attributions         |
| Version or interpretation | Nothing by itself: it is revised for another purpose or occasion                                       | Mendelssohn's 1829 cuts      |
| Evaluative                | An argument of its own kind: for a work, a passage, a comparison, a property the other judgment misses | Kramer's ranking of af Klint |

Test: for every item the text calls a mistake, error, or correction, ask what would show it was wrong, and file it accordingly. Restoring omitted music establishes greater completeness, and stops there:

> The cuts do not by themselves show that Mendelssohn was wrong. They show that
> a revival, too, gives the audience a particular interpretation of the work, and that
> the interpretation can later be reconsidered.

Reception is evidence, and never by itself a verdict. The rule:

> Popularity or survival does not, by itself, establish quality. Its
> evidential weight depends on whether the processes producing that
> reception give us reason to connect it to the particular quality being
> judged.

The distinction runs between evidence and verdict, and covers every kind of evidence equally. Reception, adoption, or an outcome can be indirect evidence about something else, but the connection has to be justified: say what process produced the reception and why that process tracks the quality in question. Test: for every popularity, survival, or success fact offered as support, ask whether the text says why the process behind it bears on the particular quality being judged. Where it does not, the fact shows only what happened to the work, and says nothing about what it is worth.

> Later attention, institutional recognition, and attendance can show that
> Kramer's judgment did not prevail; nothing in such a text connects them to
> the quality he judged. Either give the evaluative reason, or make the
> narrower historical claim: "Kramer's judgment did not come to determine her
> later reception."

Adding "I think" identifies the speaker without supplying the reason. An essay that contests an appeal to posterity does not settle its own disputes by one.

## 7. Norms the text asserts are scoped and met by the text

Test 1: for every duty, obligation, or "must" the text asserts, say who is bound, in what situation, and what it does not require. Test 2: check that the text meets that standard wherever it applies to the text itself.

> The duty to advocate binds someone making a public claim on a work's
> behalf. It is not a duty to campaign for everything one enjoys, and
> "testing" need not mean scientific reproducibility: a comparison, a described
> passage, or a performance can make a judgment available for scrutiny.
> A text that demands accountable advocacy and then asserts "Kramer was wrong about
> her rank" without scrutiny fails its own standard.

## 8. The ending keeps the distinctions the body drew

A conclusion compresses; it must not compress away a distinction the text established, or let back in a reading the text ruled out. Test: read the closing section against each distinction the body drew, and ask whether any word in it lets the collapsed reading back in.

> An ending line praising "the hand that could have struck every sentence and refrained" reduces
> authorship to the power to delete, and reopens the distinction between author and
> legally responsible publisher that the essay had drawn.
>
> A verdict that groups Mendelssohn's cuts with Thoré's attributions and
> Kramer's ranking under "the corrections" (§6), or compresses "this
> performance depended on these decisions" into "without these people the
> work would not have reached us" ([working_with_sources.md](working_with_sources.md) § 8), lets back in what the body ruled out.

Retelling the cases to restate the thesis is repetition ([cut_padding.md](../../../../core/packages/basics/rules/cut_padding.md)); spend those words on the limits of the claim instead.

## 9. The title names the claim the text can defend

Test: read the title on its weakest and on its strongest natural reading. The weak reading must not be trivially true, and the strong reading must not exceed what the argument supports. The same test applies to the thesis sentence and the standfirst.

> "Time's verdict is no verdict" reads either as nearly trivial (time adds no
> judgment of its own beyond people's) or as too strong (historical
> reception carries no evidential weight). "Time's verdict is not the final verdict"
> targets what the essay can contest: the idea that survival ends the need
> for judgment.

Exception: a title may carry the stronger reading when the standfirst narrows it explicitly. The standfirst must state the narrower claim the essay defends; a narrowing first met in the body or the ending does not count.

Label headings ("Sources and notes") and the quoted titles of other works are outside the test.

## How to apply

Run 1–3 on every passage that characterizes or rebuts another author; 4 whenever a criterion or test is proposed; 5 before any case is dropped or swapped; 6 on every word of the error family (mistake, wrong, corrected, and their equivalents in the text's language); 7 on every asserted duty; 8 and 9 last, on the closing section, the title, and the standfirst.
