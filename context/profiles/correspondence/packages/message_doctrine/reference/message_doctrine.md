# The message doctrine

Every message, whatever its channel, is written for one recipient who will decide what to do with it. The rules below hold for emails, messages, letters, replies in a thread and answers to posed questions alike. They apply the base model and the general claims doctrine to that situation and restate neither: the frame-before-detail order is in [whole_to_detail.md](../../../../core/packages/whole_to_detail/reference/whole_to_detail.md), the placement tiers in [reader_order.md](../../../../core/packages/whole_to_detail/rules/reader_order.md), statement types and calibrated confidence in [claims_and_evidence.md](../../../../core/packages/claims/reference/claims_and_evidence.md), and the stating register in [tone_and_register.md](../../../../core/packages/basics/reference/tone_and_register.md). Each rule is a test with its exception, per the base README's "tests, not bans".

The doctrine generalizes the core of a sales team's communication principles for messages of every kind. How a sales message applies it — cold first contact, offers and prices, introductions made to sell, follow-up and booked meetings — is stated in the `sales_outbound` profile, which governs sales outbound. A conflict between the two goes to whoever owns the writing rules.

## 1. The goal is a motivated next step

A message gives the recipient reasons to understand its content, take it seriously, and do something rational in their situation: answer a question, correct a fact, look at an attachment, involve the right person, or make a decision. The test: after reading, can the recipient see what step, if any, the message asks of them, and why that step makes sense from where they stand? A reaction obtained at any price fails the test, and so does a message that leaves the recipient unsure what it was for.

Three distinctions keep the test honest. Attention, understanding, trust in a specific claim and readiness to act are four different states; a read message is not a believed one, and a believed one is not a mandate. A clear no is a legitimate outcome, because it spares both sides work. And a message cannot compensate for a missing reason to write: when the recipient has no stake in the content, better wording does not supply one.

Exception: a message that only informs (a confirmation, a delivery note, a thank-you) asks for no step, and passes when it says so by asking for none.

## 2. The recipient's decision comes first

Draft the purpose before the text: "after this message, the recipient must be able to judge …". The first sentence then carries what is most relevant to the recipient in this situation — the answer, the decision the message concerns, the deviation and its consequence, or the observation that makes the topic theirs. The test is the base review test's first question at message scale: can the recipient tell from the first sentence what the message is for? A warm-up, a restatement of the subject, or an account of the sender's own activity placed first fails it.

The rule concerns information, and a pronoun alone decides nothing: a first sentence may begin with "I" when what follows is the information the recipient needs first. A sender-centric opening fails because of what it carries; the repair changes the information the opening carries, and a more elegant synonym for the same information repairs nothing.

Exception: when the recipient has asked what the sender is doing, the sender's own news is the answer and opens the reply.

## 3. Choose information before words

A well-written message with the wrong content is still wrong, so content and relationship are decided before any sentence is worded. Each item the message carries answers three questions: which of the recipient's questions it answers, which uncertainty it reduces, and why it is needed now. An item that answers none of them leaves.

Each candidate item takes one of four placements, adapted from the base tiers in [reader_order.md](../../../../core/packages/whole_to_detail/rules/reader_order.md) to a message:

- Say now: the recipient needs it to judge the relevance, the claim, a material limitation or the next step.
- Make available: some recipients need more depth; the message states the key conclusion and links or attaches the rest.
- Save for later: it belongs to a later decision and a later message.
- Omit: it is irrelevant, unverified, unnecessarily private or misleading.

A material limitation is never saved or omitted when its absence makes the present message misleading. "Later" is acceptable only for a fact that does not change the decision being asked for now. This is the message-scale form of the base purpose bar and of shortening without false impressions ([claims_and_evidence.md](../../../../core/packages/claims/reference/claims_and_evidence.md) §7 and §10).

For an important message, a few lines per candidate item help the choice: what the item is, its ground (observation, the recipient's own statement, interpretation, recommendation, commitment), its source and currency, the recipient question it answers, the wrong conclusion the recipient might otherwise draw, and its placement. This is a selection aid, never a record the message must carry.

## 4. Information order is a decision

No single order fits every message. A posed question opens with the answer; a message about a decision opens with what the decision concerns; a delay or a problem opens with the deviation and its consequence; a first contact opens with why the topic concerns the recipient. The test: for this message and this recipient, does the first thing read make the rest interpretable? The funnel in [reader_order.md](../../../../core/packages/whole_to_detail/rules/reader_order.md) governs the order after the opening.

How a sales first contact opens, including the correction of the sender-centric standard opening, is stated in the `sales_outbound` profile.

## 5. Build on the relationship that actually exists

A message builds on what has actually happened between the parties: what each knows about the other, what was last asked, declined or agreed, and what the recipient can actually decide. "As we discussed" is written only after a discussion; a polite reply is not a mandate for more. Trust is topic-specific, so a recipient's trust in one of the sender's abilities does not carry over to another unexamined.

Before a new or important message, read what the person last wrote, and work from that; an older summary is not enough when the latest reply changed the situation. Keep what was observed apart from what is interpreted: an unsupported reading of the recipient's motives or feelings never steers the message as if it were fact. Reading the same text twice — once for its facts and once for the relationship it invites — is the way to find plausible misreadings; it is not a basis for a psychological portrait.

The same information reads differently across relationships. Wording that fits an unknown recipient, a recipient after a clarification, and a client inside a paid engagement rests on different grounds, and a sentence is never moved from one situation to another because it sounds more competent there.

## 6. Precision about what is known

A message keeps its grounds apart: a verified observation, the recipient's own statement, the sender's interpretation, a recommendation, and a commitment are different kinds of statement and are written as such. Uncertainty is placed where it actually is, in the sentence it concerns, and a message made vague throughout to cover one uncertain point fails. The general rule is [claims_and_evidence.md](../../../../core/packages/claims/reference/claims_and_evidence.md) §2–3; in a message it matters most for commitments, which the recipient will hold the sender to.

## 7. A peer, and the recipient in control

The sender writes as a peer: neither a supplicant nor a superior seller. A recommendation to the recipient carries its reason, the key trade-off and what would change it, so the recipient can judge it; respecting the recipient does not mean confirming every premise they hold, and calling their process bad without grounds or a complicated task easy fails the peer test.

The recipient keeps control of the next step. Deadlines are stated only when they are real, and then with their reason; alternatives are honest; a no is made easy to give; an explicit request to end contact is respected. Engineered guilt, fear and social obligation are out, and a first yes never hides an order for more contact than the message states.

A step is asked for only when the groundwork motivates it, and the cost of answering is kept as low as the content allows: the time, the information the recipient must share, the people they must consult, the risk of a wrong yes. A step that is the other person's to take is left to them; the message offers what makes it easier.

## 8. Credibility through testability and consistency

Credibility comes from what the recipient can check: relevant work shown, limitations stated, and what was promised delivered. Words such as "leading" or "premium" are no evidence, per [show_qualities.md](../../../../core/packages/claims/rules/show_qualities.md). Every channel the recipient may compare — the message, a website, an attachment, an earlier message, the work itself — describes the same ability and the same responsibility.

## 9. Short without becoming skimpy

Every sentence has a function, and orientation and tone are functions. Readability is not a minimum word count: a message carrying more risk or more dependencies may need more text, and a message cut until the recipient cannot act has failed. The per-sentence cut test is [claims_and_evidence.md](../../../../core/packages/claims/reference/claims_and_evidence.md) §6; density by medium is in [tone_and_register.md](../../../../core/packages/basics/reference/tone_and_register.md).

## 10. The working order

Wording comes after the content and relationship decisions. The order runs intent, relationship, groundwork, information selection, next step, wording, review, outcome:

1. Intent: why this message is needed, in terms of the recipient's decision and the sender's legitimate purpose. With no contribution relevant to the recipient, no expected response and no necessary request, the right message is none until the reason is better; the sender's own need is not by itself a reason for the recipient to read.
2. Relationship: what the person actually last said, asked or declined, and the most important uncertainty now (§5).
3. Groundwork: what is known, what is assumed, and what is not known, kept apart (§6).
4. Information selection: the four placements, with the most important limitation noted so that it survives shortening (§3).
5. Next step: one main action, or none. Text is the default channel; a call or a meeting is proposed when it concretely helps shared understanding, and decisions made there are summarized in writing afterwards. The recipient must be able to take the step, and it must hide no larger work than the message implies.
6. Wording: the subject line and first sentence as one coherent orientation; then only the context and reasons the step needs; the message ends with the step when there is one. No automatic presentation of the sender and no merits paragraph.
7. Review: read as the recipient and as a sceptical third party, sentence by sentence for function, relevance, ground, subtext, friction and whether it could be removed, then as a whole, because individually reasonable sentences can add up to an overloaded text. The checks are in [pre_send_checks.md](../rules/pre_send_checks.md).
8. Outcome: record only what is needed to follow the matter and keep promises, with what was believed kept apart from what the recipient actually did. The next message starts from the actual outcome.

The effort scales with the message. A confirmation needs a quick check; a first contact needs the relationship and the information selection; a security answer, a dispute or a delivery problem needs more. The order exists to prevent unconsidered messages, and it does not turn every message into a project.
